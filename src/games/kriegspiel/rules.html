A Game of War sets two armies, North and South, on a board of 25 columns by 20 rows. Each side tries to defeat the other by destroying both of its arsenals or by eliminating all of its combat units. Everything depends on lines of communication: a unit that is no longer linked to one of its arsenals can neither move, attack, nor defend itself.

Terrain

  • Arsenal (2 per side): the source of the lines of communication. The square itself is plain ground.
  • Mountain: impassable; it stops fire and lines of communication.
  • Mountain pass: the only way through a mountain range; it strengthens the defence of the unit holding it.
  • Fort (3 per territory): it strengthens the defence of the unit inside, whichever side it belongs to. A fort is never destroyed.

Deployment

North first places its 17 units anywhere in its territory (the ten rows A to J), then South does the same in its own (rows K to T). One unit per square, never on a mountain; forts, the pass and arsenals are allowed. North then plays the first turn.

In the panel, pick a unit type and click a square; clicking a unit already placed takes it back. "Automatic placement" proposes a complete deployment.

The "Blind" button

In Debord's game each side deploys without seeing the other. This button restores that secrecy when two players share one screen. It sits in the panel during North's deployment and toggles between "no" (the starting setting) and "yes".

  • On "yes": while South places its units, North's units and lines of communication are not displayed. They reappear as soon as South has confirmed its deployment. Since North placed before South, neither has seen the other's deployment — provided South does not look at the screen while North is placing.
  • On "no": South sees North's deployment and may adapt to it.

This only hides things on screen: the rules, the saved game and the notation are unchanged, and nothing is hidden once play has begun. The setting applies to the current game and is chosen before North's deployment is confirmed. Against the computer it changes nothing: the computer always deploys without taking the opposing deployment into account.

A turn

On their turn a player may, in this order:

  1. carry out a forced retreat, if one is due (see below);
  2. move up to five units, the retreat included, each of them once only;
  3. make one attack at most.

Nothing is compulsory except the retreat: a player may pass. The attack closes the turn: once the target is chosen, no unit moves any more. "End turn" sends the move; "Undo last" takes back the attack or the last move.

Communications

  • Each arsenal still standing sends out lines of communication in the eight directions, with no limit of length.
  • A line runs through empty squares, friendly units, forts, passes and enemy communications units. It stops in front of a mountain and in front of any other enemy unit.
  • A friendly communications unit standing on a line sends out eight lines of its own, and so on.
  • Any unit standing on a line is in communication. A combat unit touching (in the eight directions) a friendly combat unit in communication is in communication as well, from one to the next.
  • A communications unit is in communication only when it stands on a line: contact is not enough for it, and it passes nothing on by contact.

An isolated unit can neither move nor attack, and its defence is nil; it can still be defended by its neighbours in communication. Only communications units may move while isolated. Communications are worked out again after every move, so the order of the moves matters.

Movement

A unit moves as many squares as its movement allows, one step at a time in the eight directions, through empty squares only: it crosses neither mountains, nor friendly units, nor enemy units. It may end its move out of communication.

Arsenals

A combat unit entering an empty enemy arsenal destroys it, and stops there. That destruction stands for the attack of the turn. A communications unit does not destroy an arsenal. If the arsenal is occupied, its occupant has to be driven out first.

Combat

Any enemy unit may be attacked. Fire follows the eight straight lines through the target's square; a mountain stops it, units and passes do not.

  • Attack: add up the attack factors of the attacker's combat units that are in communication, in line with the target and within range.
  • Charge: a cavalry unit next to the target counts 7 instead of 4, and so does each cavalry unit following it without a gap on the same line, up to four. There is no charge against a target in a fort or in a pass, nor for a cavalry unit that is itself in a fort.
  • Defence: add up the defence factors of the target and of the units of its side that are in communication, in line with it and within range. Fort and pass bonuses count for the unit standing there.

The two totals are then compared:

  • attack lower than or equal to defence: the attack has no effect (it is not offered);
  • attack higher by 1: forced retreat;
  • attack higher by 2 or more: the unit is destroyed. The attacker does not move.

A unit forced to retreat must move first on its side's next turn, using its normal movement; that move counts among the five, and the unit takes no part in that turn's attack. If it cannot withdraw (isolated, or no free square), it is destroyed.

Hover over a unit to read the balance of forces and each unit's share. Possible targets are marked in red (destruction) or orange (retreat).

End of the game

A side wins when its opponent:

  • has lost both arsenals;
  • or has no combat unit left;
  • or has no communications unit left while all its remaining units are isolated (optional rule, on by default).

Losing both communications units is therefore not enough to lose: as long as one unit remains in communication (on an arsenal's line, or touching a unit that is), the game goes on. The panel shows at all times how many units of each side are in communication.

The game is drawn after 400 moves, or when the same position occurs three times.
