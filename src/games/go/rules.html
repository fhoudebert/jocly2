Go (also weiqi, baduk or igo) is played on the intersections of a grid. Black plays first. On your turn you either place one stone on any empty intersection, or pass.

Groups and capture

Stones of the same colour that touch along a line — not a diagonal — form a group. The empty intersections next to a group are its liberties. A group with no liberty left is captured and removed from the board, all of it at once.

Captures are resolved in the opponent's favour first: when you place a stone, any enemy group left without a liberty is taken off before your own stone is checked. This is why you may fill your last liberty when doing so captures.

Choosing the rules

Before the first stone, the board asks which rule set to play: Chinese (OGS) or Tromp-Taylor. The answer is recorded as part of the game, so a saved game replays under the rules it was played under, and the engine is told about it too.

The two agree on almost everything: area scoring, positional superko, no tax on eyes in seki. They differ on one point, self-capture, described below. Anything the rest of this page says holds under both unless it says otherwise.

“Chinese (OGS)” rather than plain “Chinese”: the Chinese rules used in tournaments settle repetitions differently. What is played here is the reading used by the OGS and KGS servers, and it is the one the engine is asked to follow.

Suicide

Under Chinese (OGS) you may not place a stone that would leave its own group without a liberty, unless the same move captures. A single-point eye of your own colour is therefore not a legal move for you, while it may well be one for your opponent.

Under Tromp-Taylor the same move is legal when it takes more than one stone off the board — your new stone and the group it joins go together, and count as prisoners for your opponent. Filling your own last liberty with a lone stone stays illegal in both. Self-capture is rarely useful, but it is occasionally the cheapest way to end a ko fight or to fill your own territory under time pressure.

Ko

You may not play a move that recreates a position the game has already been in. In practice this shows up as the ko: after one stone is captured, the opponent cannot take straight back, and must play elsewhere first. The wider rule — called positional superko — also covers the rarer repetitions that the simple ko does not. Both rule sets use it, so the ko behaves the same way whichever you chose.

Reading the bar

The bar above the board shows, beside each colour, the number of stones that colour has captured so far, and between them the komi. Captures are not the score — under area rules they only matter through the stones they remove — but they are the one number that is a fact while the game is running.

Once both players have passed, the same two places show the final score, and the middle names the winner and by how much.

Ending the game and scoring

The game ends when both players pass in succession. Both rule sets count by area, so the counting is the same either way: your score is the number of your stones on the board plus the number of empty intersections that only your colour reaches. An empty region touching both colours is neutral and counts for nobody. Prisoners do not enter the score — under area rules they matter only through the stones they removed.

White adds komi to compensate for playing second — 7.5 points on a 19x19 board, 6.5 on 13x13, 5.5 on 9x9. The half point makes a draw impossible.

One consequence is worth knowing before you pass. There is no separate step for agreeing which stones are dead: the board is counted exactly as it stands. Stones you consider dead but have not captured still count for your opponent, so play the position out before passing.
